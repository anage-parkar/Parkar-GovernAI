Foundation — Section 1

What Is AI Governance?

Before touching any software, understand the concept. AI Governance is the answer to a very specific business problem that every organisation using AI faces today.

The Problem in Plain Terms

Imagine your company uses AI in 10 different places — a chatbot, a fraud detector, a hiring tool, a loan approval system, a medical diagnosis aid. Each one makes decisions that affect real people. Now ask yourself these questions:

  • Can you tell a regulator exactly what AI systems you run and what they do?
  • If one of them discriminates against a minority group, do you know about it?
  • If your AI makes a wrong decision and someone is harmed — who is responsible?
  • Can you prove, with documents and evidence, that you assessed the risks before deploying?
  • Is there a written policy governing how your team builds and uses AI?
  • When something goes wrong, do you have a process to report it to regulators?

The harsh reality: Most organisations today cannot answer YES to most of these questions. They use AI extensively but have zero documented governance. Under the EU AI Act, that is now a legal violation with fines up to €35 million or 7% of global annual turnover.

What AI Governance Actually Is

AI Governance is the set of processes, policies, controls, and evidence that prove your organisation is using AI responsibly. Think of it like financial accounting — just as you track every dollar and prove to auditors that your finances are sound, AI governance tracks every AI system and proves to regulators that your AI is safe, fair, and compliant.

Foundation — Section 2

Why Parkar GovernAI Exists

The problem is not new — but the urgency is. Three things converged to make AI governance a business-critical priority right now.

1. Regulation Has Arrived

The EU AI Act came into force in August 2024 — the world's first comprehensive AI law. It applies to ANY organisation that deploys AI systems affecting EU citizens, regardless of where the organisation is based. Penalties are severe and enforcement is already beginning.

2. AI Is Everywhere and Out of Control

The average enterprise runs hundreds of AI systems — many deployed by individual teams without central oversight. Nobody knows the full inventory. Nobody tracks the risks. Nobody has the evidence. This is the shadow AI problem.

3. Spreadsheets Don't Work at Scale

The traditional approach is spreadsheets — one for risk, one for models, one for policies. They get outdated, they're not connected, they produce no evidence trail, and they fail every audit. Parkar GovernAI replaces the entire spreadsheet ecosystem with a single, connected platform.

What Parkar GovernAI solves: It gives organisations a single system of record for every AI governance activity — from registering an AI system to generating a regulator-ready compliance report — eliminating spreadsheets, email chains, and the risk of non-compliance.

Foundation — Section 3

Regulatory Frameworks Explained Simply

You don't need to be a lawyer to understand these. Here is what each framework actually means in plain English — and how Parkar GovernAI maps to each one.

🇪🇺 EU Law
EU AI Act
The world's first binding AI law. Classifies AI systems by risk level. High-risk systems (loans, hiring, medical) must be documented, risk-assessed, and transparent. Serious incidents must be reported to regulators.
🌐 ISO Standard
ISO 42001
The international standard for AI Management Systems. Like ISO 9001 for quality — it tells you HOW to build an AI governance program. 38 clauses + 93 annex controls covering everything from leadership commitment to incident management.
🌐 ISO Standard
ISO 27001
The international standard for Information Security Management. AI systems process sensitive data — ISO 27001 ensures that data is protected. Often required alongside ISO 42001 for complete AI governance.
🇺🇸 US Framework
NIST AI RMF
The US National Institute of Standards voluntary AI Risk Management Framework. 4 functions: Govern (set up the program), Map (identify AI risks), Measure (analyse them), Manage (treat and monitor). Widely adopted globally.

Risk Management

The central AI risk register. Import from IBM/MIT databases, score, assign, and track mitigation.

/risk-management

Most organisations start AI risk assessment with a blank spreadsheet. Parkar GovernAI gives you two curated databases — IBM (113 risks) and MIT (academic research) — so your risk register is populated in minutes, not weeks.

Risk Level Calculation Formula

Risk Level = (Likelihood × 1) + (Severity × 3)
Severity is weighted 3× because a catastrophic but rare event must still be prioritised over a frequent low-severity one. This mirrors EU AI Act risk methodology.

Clarity & Concepts — Section 1

Your Biggest Question: Is AI Governance Just Documentation?

YES — and that is exactly the point. Understanding this one thing will make everything else click instantly.

Short answer: Parkar GovernAI is a Management and Documentation System, NOT a deployment or execution system. You do not run AI through it, connect AI models to it, or integrate it with production systems via API keys. It is where you RECORD and PROVE that you are governing your AI responsibly.

The Perfect Analogy: Financial Accounting

Think of it this way. A company earns revenue, pays employees, buys equipment — all of that happens in their actual business systems (POS terminals, payroll software, bank accounts). But they also maintain an accounting system where they record, document, and prove every financial transaction — not to run the business, but to prove to auditors, tax authorities, and investors that the money is being managed correctly.

Parkar GovernAI is the accounting system for your AI. Your AI runs in your actual systems (your servers, OpenAI's API, AWS). Parkar GovernAI is where you record, document, and prove that your AI is being governed correctly.

What Parkar GovernAI IS vs IS NOT
✅ What It IS
  • A registry of all your AI systems
  • A documentation vault for compliance evidence
  • A risk register tracking what could go wrong
  • A compliance tracker for ISO/EU AI Act controls
  • A policy library for written governance rules
  • An incident log for when things go wrong
  • A report generator for regulators and boards
  • A transparency portal for public trust
❌ What It IS NOT
  • A platform that runs your AI models
  • A place to enter API keys to connect to OpenAI (that's AI Gateway)
  • A system that trains your machine learning models
  • A tool that monitors AI performance in real time
  • A deployment pipeline for AI systems
  • A system that stores your actual datasets (the CSVs)
  • An AI model testing or evaluation environment

The Regulator Analogy

When a regulator (e.g., the EU AI Act enforcement authority) audits your organisation, they don't plug into your servers to check your AI. They ask you to show them documents:

  • "Show me the list of AI systems you operate" → Use Cases page
  • "Show me the risk assessment for your loan approval AI" → Risk Management page
  • "Show me the training data governance documentation" → Datasets page
  • "Show me evidence that you assessed the model's security" → Evidence Hub
  • "Show me the incident you reported last month" → Incident Management
  • "Show me your AI governance policy" → Policy Manager

Parkar GovernAI is the system that holds ALL of those documents, organised, versioned, linked together, and ready to export as a PDF in 30 seconds.

The ONE exception — Agent Discovery: This is the only module that actually connects to an external system (Azure AI Foundry, AWS Bedrock) to pull a live inventory of AI agents. Everything else is manual documentation and record-keeping.

The Complete Connected Flow — "Loan Approval AI" Example

Let's trace one real AI system through every module to show exactly how they all connect and why each piece matters.

The scenario: Your bank uses GPT-4o to evaluate loan applications. It was trained on 5 years of customer loan history. A vendor (OpenAI) provides the API. The system is High Risk under EU AI Act. Something goes wrong one day — it starts rejecting applications incorrectly. Here is how Parkar GovernAI handles the entire lifecycle.

Step 1 — Use Case: Register the AI System

You create a Use Case called "Loan Approval AI" (auto-gets UC-1). You classify it as High Risk under EU AI Act. This single record becomes the anchor for everything else.

Why: EU AI Act requires you to enumerate every High-Risk AI system. UC-1 is your regulatory reference number. If a regulator asks "what AI systems do you have?" — you show this list.

Step 2 — Model Inventory: Register GPT-4o

You add GPT-4o to the Model Inventory. Provider: OpenAI. You upload a security assessment PDF. Status changes from Pending → Approved. You link it to UC-1 (Loan Approval AI).

Why: EU AI Act Article 13 requires technical documentation for High-Risk AI models. The security assessment PDF is the evidence. The approval status is the governance gate — no unapproved model should be in production. The link to UC-1 means "this model powers that use case".

Step 3 — Datasets: Document the Training Data

You add a Dataset record: "Customer Loan History 2019–2024". You do NOT upload the actual 500GB CSV file. You record: Type = Training, Classification = Restricted, Contains PII = Yes, Known Biases = historical demographic disparities, Bias Mitigation = stratified sampling applied. You link it to GPT-4o.

Why: EU AI Act Article 10 requires data governance documentation for High-Risk AI. You're proving to a regulator "we know what data trained this model, we know it contains PII, we know the biases, and we took steps to mitigate them." The link to GPT-4o creates the chain: Training Data → Model → Use Case.

Step 4 — Vendors: Assess OpenAI as a Supplier

You add OpenAI as a vendor. Data Sensitivity = PII (loan data goes to their API). Business Criticality = High. Regulatory Exposure = EU AI Act + GDPR. The scorecard auto-calculates a high risk score. You add a Vendor Risk: "EU customer data processed on US servers — GDPR transfer risk."

Why: EU AI Act Article 28 says if you deploy a third-party AI, you're responsible for documenting that relationship. You can't just say "OpenAI handles it." You must document: what data they receive, how critical they are, what risks that creates, and what you're doing about it.

Step 5 — Risk Management: Import and Assess Risks

From the IBM database you import "Biased decision outputs" (linked to UC-1) and "Hallucinatory incorrect output" (what actually happens in the incident). You assign owners, set mitigation plans, add deadlines.

Why: EU AI Act Article 9 requires a risk management system with documented risks and treatments. These risks are now linked to UC-1 (Loan Approval AI) — when a regulator asks "did you assess the risks for this system?", you show this register with owners, statuses, and mitigation plans.

Step 6 — Frameworks: Map to ISO 42001 Controls

In the Frameworks page, you open Clause 8.2 (AI Risk Assessment) and mark it Implemented. You attach the Risk Assessment as evidence. Clause 7.2 (Competence) is marked Implemented because you added training records.

Why: ISO 42001 certification requires you to demonstrate that specific controls are in place. Each control you mark as Implemented, backed by evidence, is a checkbox ticked toward certification. The evidence you attached is what an auditor would review during a certification audit.

Step 7 — Training Registry: Prove Your Team Is Trained

You add a training record: "EU AI Act Compliance for Financial Services Teams — 24 people — Completed." Certificate PDF uploaded to Evidence Hub and linked.

Why: EU AI Act Article 4 says your organisation must ensure AI literacy. ISO 42001 Clause 7.2 says you need documented evidence of competence. This record, with the certificate, IS that evidence. It links back to ISO 42001 Clause 7.2 (Competence) being Implemented.

Step 8 — Evidence Hub: Collect All Proof Documents

You upload: Security Assessment Report (linked to GPT-4o model), Model Card (linked to GPT-4o), Bias and Fairness Report (linked to GPT-4o and UC-1). Each links to the specific framework controls it satisfies.

Why: Evidence is the PROOF behind every claim. When you mark "ISO 42001 Clause 8.2 = Implemented", an auditor asks "prove it." The linked Risk Assessment Report IS the proof. Without evidence, a compliance control is just a checkbox with no backing.

Step 9 — Policy Manager: Write the Governance Rule

You write an "AI Risk Management Policy" using a template. It goes through Draft → Under Review → Approved. You link it to the risk "Biased decision outputs" and to ISO 42001 Clause 5.2 (AI Policy).

Why: Compliance frameworks require WRITTEN policies. "We manage AI risk" is not enough — you need a documented, approved policy that says HOW. The link to the risk means "this policy addresses that risk." The link to the framework control means "this policy satisfies that control."

Step 10 — Incident Management: Log the Failure

The model starts incorrectly rejecting 340 applications. You create Incident INC-0001: Type = Unexpected behavior, Severity = Serious, Harm = Fundamental Rights + Financial Impact. You document the mitigation (rollback to v2.2) and corrective actions.

Why: EU AI Act Article 73 requires MANDATORY reporting of serious incidents to the regulator. INC-0001 is the regulatory filing. Without this record, you have no proof you discovered, investigated, and reported the incident — that itself is a violation.

Step 11 — Reporting: Generate the Proof Package

You click Generate Report → Scope: Loan Approval AI → Framework: EU AI Act → All sections → PDF. In 30 seconds, a branded document compiles everything above into a single regulator-ready file.

Why: This is the deliverable. Every audit, board meeting, or regulatory submission needs a document. The report is a snapshot of everything you've done — all in one place, formatted professionally, under your organisation's branding.

Datasets — What You Are Actually Adding

This is the most confusing module for new users. You are NOT uploading the actual data files. You are documenting metadata about the data.

The Library Catalogue Analogy

A library has thousands of books. The librarian doesn't store the full text of every book in the catalogue system — they record the title, author, genre, location, and condition. The actual books are on the shelves. The catalogue is how you prove the library exists and how you find what you need.

Parkar GovernAI's Datasets module is the catalogue for your AI training data. Your actual dataset (a 500GB CSV of loan applications) lives on your data warehouse, S3 bucket, or database. In Parkar GovernAI, you record the catalogue entry: what the dataset is, where it comes from, how sensitive it is, and what biases it contains.

What You ARE and ARE NOT Adding
✅ What You ARE Adding
  • The name of the dataset
  • The type (Training / Validation / Testing)
  • The source (which system it came from)
  • The classification (Restricted/Confidential/Public)
  • Whether it contains PII and what types
  • Known biases in the data
  • Bias mitigation steps taken
  • The function (what it was used for)
❌ What You ARE NOT Adding
  • The actual CSV/Excel/JSON file
  • The actual rows of data
  • A database connection string
  • The data itself for analysis

Why This Matters for Compliance

EU AI Act Article 10 requires you to document your training, validation, and testing data — not upload it. The regulator wants to know:

  • Did you know what data trained your model? → Source and Name fields
  • Was the data sensitive? → Classification and PII fields
  • Were there known biases? → Known Biases field
  • What did you do about those biases? → Bias Mitigation field
  • How does this data link to which model? → Linked Models field

Real example of what you enter:
Name: "Customer Loan Applications 2019–2024"
Type: Training
Contains PII: Yes — National ID, Income, Address, Credit Score
Known Biases: "Historical approval rate disparities between demographic groups 2019–2021"
Bias Mitigation: "Applied stratified sampling. Removed direct demographic identifiers from feature set."

Evidence is not just storage. Every document you upload links to something specific and serves a specific purpose. Here is exactly where evidence flows after upload.

Core concept: Evidence is the PROOF behind every claim. When you say "we assessed the model's security" — the Security Assessment Report PDF is the evidence. When you say "ISO 42001 Clause 8.2 is Implemented" — the Risk Assessment Report attached to that control is the evidence. Without evidence, compliance controls are just checkbox theatre.

The 4 Places Evidence Gets Linked

1. Linked to a Model (in Model Inventory → Evidence Hub tab)

When you upload a Security Assessment Report and link it to GPT-4o, it proves that GPT-4o specifically was assessed. The model's Evidence Hub tab becomes its compliance dossier — everything you need to justify that model being Approved.

A policy like "AI Risk Management Policy" can link to evidence documents that support it — e.g., a third-party audit report that validates the policy is being followed.

4. Linked to a Training Program (in Training Registry → Evidence Hub tab)

Training completion certificates, attendance records, and assessment results upload here, linked to the training program. This is the proof that your employees actually attended and passed training — not just that the training was scheduled.

Clarity & Concepts — Section 7

Model Risk vs Vendor Risk — The Clear Difference

These two types of risk look similar but come from fundamentally different sources. One is about what the AI does. The other is about who supplies it.

Side-by-Side Comparison
⚠️ Model Risk

Definition: A risk that comes from the AI model itself — how it behaves, what it gets wrong, what it can be tricked into doing.

Source: The model's design, training, or behaviour

Location: Model Inventory → Model Risks tab

Examples for GPT-4o:

  • Demographic bias in outputs (the model discriminates)
  • Hallucinations (invents false information)
  • Prompt injection (can be manipulated by malicious input)
  • Model drift (degrades over time as data distribution shifts)
  • Context window limits causing incomplete analysis
🏢 Vendor Risk

Definition: A risk that comes from your RELATIONSHIP with the vendor supplying the model — not the model itself but the business, legal, and operational relationship.

Source: The vendor's business practices, legal status, or reliability

Location: Vendors → Vendor Risks tab

Examples for OpenAI as vendor:

  • EU customer data processed on US servers (GDPR transfer risk)
  • OpenAI service outage → your loan system goes down (operational risk)
  • OpenAI changes pricing → your costs spike unexpectedly (financial risk)
  • OpenAI deprecates the API version you use (vendor lock-in risk)
  • OpenAI suffers a data breach (security risk from vendor)

The One-Line Difference

Model Risk: "What could the AI model itself do wrong?" — focuses on the model's technical behaviour.
Vendor Risk: "What could go wrong with the company that provides the model?" — focuses on the business relationship.

A Simple Test to Tell Them Apart

Ask yourself: "If we switched to a different AI model from the same vendor, would this risk still exist?"

  • YES → It is a Vendor Risk (e.g., data transfer to US servers exists regardless of which OpenAI model you use)
  • NO → It is a Model Risk (e.g., GPT-4o's hallucination behaviour is specific to that model — a different model might not have the same issue)

Training Registry — Why It Exists and What "Training" Means Here

Important: "Training" in this module means training YOUR PEOPLE — not training your AI model. This is completely separate from machine learning model training.

What the Training Registry Tracks

It tracks educational and awareness programs for your employees — courses, workshops, certifications, and awareness sessions that teach your team how to use AI responsibly and comply with AI regulations.

Two Completely Different Things Called "Training"
📚 Training Registry (People)

Teaching employees how to govern and use AI responsibly.

Examples:

  • EU AI Act Compliance course for Legal team
  • AI Ethics workshop for all employees
  • ISO 42001 certification training for governance team
  • Bias awareness training for data scientists
🤖 ML Model Training (NOT this)

Teaching an AI algorithm to make predictions using data.

Examples (NOT in this platform):

  • Fine-tuning GPT-4o on your loan data
  • Training a classifier on customer records
  • Running gradient descent on a neural network

Why It Is a Legal Requirement

EU AI Act Article 4 states: "Providers and deployers of AI systems shall take measures to ensure, to their best extent, a sufficient level of AI literacy of their staff."

This means you must be able to PROVE your employees received AI literacy training. The Training Registry is that proof. When an auditor asks "how do you ensure your staff understands AI risks?" — you show the training log: 24 people in Legal attended a full-day EU AI Act compliance course, here is the attendance certificate, it is linked as evidence.

How It Links to Everything Else

  • Training record → linked to ISO 42001 Clause 7.2 (Competence) as evidence that the control is Implemented
  • Training certificate PDF → uploaded to Evidence Hub under type "Training Registry Report"
  • Training completion → contributes to Governance Score (% of programs completed)
  • Expiry on certificates → appears in Dashboard "Files needing attention" section before lapsing
